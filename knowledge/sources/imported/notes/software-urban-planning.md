# Urban planning of software

## Core argument

A codebase is a city. Some districts are a pleasure to be in and you keep
coming back; others you "just want to skip through — don't stop, you don't
know what happens." Roadwork (maintenance/refactoring) is resented the way
road maintenance is: "Is it needed at all? Why does it take so much time?
There's always roadwork." The question the note poses: neglected districts in
cities arise from identifiable planning dynamics — do the same dynamics
produce derelict areas of code, and can *deliberate urban planning* prevent or
eliminate them, instead of leaving codebase geography to coincidence?

## Extracted claims

- Empirical (observation): codebases develop strongly differentiated
  "neighborhoods" — areas developers avoid vs. areas they gravitate to — and
  developers navigate around the bad ones by habit.
- Empirical (open question posed as claim-to-test): code in bad areas
  deteriorates further — avoidance itself accelerates decay (a broken-windows
  dynamic).
- Definitional: refactoring/maintenance is the roadwork of software — it
  degrades the experience locally and temporarily while (maybe) improving it
  structurally, and it is perceived identically ("why does it take so long?").
- Normative: which areas are invested in should be an *active planning
  choice*, not an emergent accident — "not by coincidence but by active
  choices."
- Open questions kept live in the note: are there analogies to slum vs. rich
  areas? what happens at the suburbs — are greenfield additions utopias or
  instant ghettos?

## Agreements and disagreements

A systemic metaphor with unusual reach: it imports an entire discipline
(urban planning) that has already studied neglect, gentrification, zoning,
and infrastructure investment under political constraints. For the framework
it suggests treating codebase health as *geography governed by investment
policy* rather than as aggregate "tech debt." Caution for fact-checking: the
underlying urban theories (e.g. broken windows) are themselves contested;
the metaphor should not smuggle in their conclusions.

## Quotable

- "Can we avoid these areas, not by coincidence but by active choices?"
- "There's always roadwork."

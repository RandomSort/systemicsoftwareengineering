# Used and Abused Figures of DevOps

## Core argument

Visualizations are great when used to enlighten and inform — but the same
figures can just as easily be used to mandate decisions and twist perception
of reality. Agile and DevOps discourse runs on charts, quadrants, and diagrams
("you can't say Agile without having drawn at least a few quadrants with axes
scaling from 'good' to 'bad'"). Many of these load-bearing visualizations are
misunderstood at best and abused at worst, and cognitive biases do the rest.
The talk's method: per figure, name the misunderstanding, the consequence, and
the correction.

## Extracted claims

- Empirical: common figures of the Agile/DevOps canon are widely
  misunderstood by the people who deploy them in arguments.
- Empirical: misused figures have real consequences — they drive
  prioritization and decision-making, not just slideware.
- Definitional: there are two distinct uses of a visualization — *informing*
  (a model offered for shared reasoning) and *mandating* (a model wielded as
  authority). The second is abuse even when the figure is accurate.
- Empirical/psychological: cognitive biases mediate the abuse; checking the
  figure means checking the bias behind its use.
- Normative: every recurring figure in a methodology deserves a critical
  review of what it actually shows, what population it came from, and what it
  cannot support.

## Agreements and disagreements

This is Goodhart's-law-adjacent but distinct: not "metrics get gamed" but
"models get weaponized." For a systemic framework it suggests a concept like
*epistemic artifacts vs. authority artifacts* — the same diagram changes
function depending on the power relation it is used within. Tension to watch:
the framework itself will produce figures; by this source's own argument they
will be abused downstream.

## Quotable

- "Visualizations are great when being used to enlighten and inform. But they
  can just as easily be used to mandate decisions and twist our perception of
  reality."
